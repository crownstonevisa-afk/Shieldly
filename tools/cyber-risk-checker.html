Cyber Risk Score Checker

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Self-assessment only • Runs in your browser — your answers are never sent or stored

Most small business cyber incidents are not sophisticated. They are a password reused one time too many, an email account with no second line of defence, a backup that turned out never to have worked, an update postponed for six months. The good news hidden inside that fact is powerful: a short list of basic habits prevents the majority of real-world losses — and it is the same short list insurers now ask about before they will quote you cyber cover. This checker asks the ten questions that matter most, scores your answers out of 100, places you in a risk band, and hands you a personalised fix list in the order a consultant would tackle it: worst gaps first, quick wins before big projects.

Why Your Score Matters

A single number does three useful things. First, it makes an invisible problem visible: owners routinely believe they are "probably fine" until they answer honestly and count five gaps they had never thought about. Second, it prioritises. Security advice fails when it arrives as a hundred-item list with no order; a score with a fix list tells you the three things to do this week that will move your risk the most. Third, it speaks the language of insurance. Cyber insurers increasingly price — and sometimes decline — cover based on exactly these controls. A business that can show multi-factor authentication, tested backups, and trained staff is a better risk, gets smoother applications, and recovers from incidents faster whether or not it ever makes a claim. Your score is not about perfection; it is about knowing where you stand and watching the number climb as you fix things.

Answer each question honestly for how your business actually runs today — not how you intend it to run. Choose Yes only if the habit is genuinely in place everywhere it matters, Partly if it is in place in some places or some of the time, and No if it is not happening. There are no trick questions, and a low score is information, not a verdict: every fix on your list is something a small business can realistically do.

1. Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

Is MFA switched on for business email and all key accounts (banking, accounting, cloud storage, social media)?

2. Password manager and unique passwords

Does everyone use a password manager with a different password for every account — no repeats, no shared logins?

3. Backups that are separate — and tested

Are your important files backed up regularly to somewhere separate (offline drive or cloud) — and have you actually tested restoring from a backup?

4. Automatic updates

Do computers, phones, and business software update automatically, or get patched within days of an update appearing?

5. Staff security training

Have you and your staff been shown how to spot phishing emails, fake invoices, and payment-diversion scams in the last 12 months?

6. Antivirus / endpoint protection

Is reputable antivirus or endpoint protection installed, active, and kept updated on every business device?

7. Firewall and separate guest Wi-Fi

Is a firewall active on your network/router, and do customers or guests use a separate Wi-Fi network from the one your business systems use?

8. Written incident response plan

Do you have a short written plan for the day something goes wrong — who to call, what to disconnect, how to keep trading?

9. Least-privilege access

Is access limited so each person (and each supplier or contractor) can only reach what they genuinely need — with admin accounts kept separate and removed when people leave?

10. Encryption on devices and data

Are laptops encrypted (BitLocker, FileVault, or similar) and sensitive customer data stored encrypted where possible?

    What Insurers Ask When Quoting Cyber Cover

    If you apply for cyber insurance, expect the application form to read like this checker. Insurers have paid out on enough small business incidents to know precisely which habits separate a scare from a catastrophe, and their questions track the claims data. They will ask whether multi-factor authentication protects email and remote access, because account takeover is the front door for most payment-diversion and data-theft losses. They will ask about backups — how often, stored where, and whether they are isolated from your main network — because a clean, tested backup is the difference between a ransomware incident and a ransomware payment. They will ask about patching and updates, endpoint protection, and staff training, since unpatched software and one clicked phishing link start a large share of incidents. Many will also ask about incident response: who you would call, and whether a plan exists before the worst day arrives.

    Practical consequence: some insurers now decline or load premiums for businesses with no MFA or no offline-capable backup, and most offer smoother terms to businesses that can answer these questions well. Working through your fix list is therefore not just safer — it measurably improves the price and availability of the cover described in our cyber insurance guide. If an application asks something and the honest answer is "no," say so: misrepresenting your controls can void a claim precisely when you need it.
