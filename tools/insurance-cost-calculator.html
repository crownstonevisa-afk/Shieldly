Business Insurance Cost Calculator

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Educational estimate only • Runs in your browser — your answers are never sent or stored

"How much does business insurance cost?" is the first question almost every owner asks — and the honest answer is that it depends on a handful of predictable factors. A solo consultant working from a laptop in Ohio simply does not present the same risk as a busy restaurant with ten staff in Florida, and premiums reflect that. This calculator turns those factors — your industry, your state, your revenue, your team size, and the policies you choose — into a clear yearly estimate range for each type of cover, so you can budget before you ever speak to an agent and recognise a fair quote when you see one.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Pick your industry as honestly as you can. Insurers rate the work itself first: cooking, building, and advising all carry different claim patterns.
  2. Select your state. Premiums, legal costs, and workers' compensation rules vary by state, so the same business can cost noticeably more in one state than another.
  3. Add your revenue band and employee count. Revenue drives liability exposure; headcount drives workers' compensation, which is priced mainly on payroll.
  4. Tick the policies you want and press the button. You will see a low–high yearly range per policy, a combined total, and the monthly equivalent — the number most owners actually budget by.

    How This Estimate Works

    The calculator starts with a base annual range for each policy in each industry — the span a typical small, low-revenue business in that trade commonly pays, based on the published educational ranges used throughout our guides. It then applies four adjustments that mirror how insurers really rate a policy. First, a revenue multiplier (from ×0.85 for under $100,000 of revenue up to ×1.9 above $5 million) scales liability, professional, property, and cyber prices, because more sales usually means more exposure. Second, a state multiplier (×0.90 in the lowest-cost states, ×1.0 in standard states, ×1.15 in higher-cost states, and ×1.30 in the most expensive, such as California, New York, and New Jersey) reflects local claim costs and state rules. Third, a small team-size factor lifts liability pricing as headcount grows. Fourth, a trading-history factor adds a modest loading for brand-new businesses and a small discount for firms trading over ten years. Workers' compensation is calculated differently — as a per-employee rate for your industry multiplied by your staff count and state tier — because that is how it is genuinely priced: on payroll and job risk, not on profit or revenue.

    The Factors That Move Your Premium

    Industry is the biggest single factor. A contractor's crew works at height around the public; a consultant's risk is a costly mistake in an email. Insurers have decades of claims data per trade, and they price what the data says. State comes next: medical costs, legal environments, and workers' compensation systems differ state by state, which is why the same coffee shop pays more in New York than in Nebraska. Revenue and payroll scale your exposure — more customers, more transactions, more chances for something to go wrong, and for workers' compensation the premium is quite literally a rate times your payroll. Finally, your own history matters in a real quote: past claims, gaps in cover, and thin safety practices all push prices up, while a clean record and documented safety habits pull them down. This calculator cannot see your history, which is one reason its output is a range rather than a price.

    Worked Example

    Imagine a small restaurant in Ohio with $300,000 of annual revenue, ten employees, and six years of trading history, buying general liability, commercial property, and workers' compensation. Ohio is a standard-cost state (×1.0) and the revenue band multiplier is ×1.0, so the industry base ranges apply almost directly: general liability of $900–$2,500 nudged up slightly by the ten-person team to about $950–$2,630; property at $1,200–$3,200; and workers' compensation at $300–$700 per employee, which for ten staff is $3,000–$7,000. The calculator totals roughly $5,150–$12,830 per year — about $430–$1,070 per month. Notice how workers' compensation is the largest line even though nobody thinks of it first: in businesses with teams, payroll-driven cover often costs more than the liability policy owners worry about.
