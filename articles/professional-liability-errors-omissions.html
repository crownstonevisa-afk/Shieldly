Professional Liability (Errors & Omissions) Insurance, Explained

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

If your business sells expertise — advice, designs, code, accounts, marketing, or any professional service — your biggest risk is not a broken window. It is a client who says your mistake cost them money and wants you to pay for it. Professional liability insurance, also called errors and omissions or E&O cover, is built for exactly that moment, funding your legal defense and any covered settlement or judgment.

This guide explains how E&O differs from general liability, why it is almost always sold on a claims-made basis, and what retroactive dates and tail cover mean for your protection. You will find realistic US cost ranges for consultants, agencies, and other professionals, plus the contract clauses that make clients insist on this policy before work begins.

What It Covers

Negligence claims

Covers allegations that you failed to meet the professional standard expected in your field, whether or not you actually made the mistake the client describes.

Errors and omissions in your work

Responds when a genuine slip — a wrong figure in a report, a missed filing date, a bug shipped to production — causes a client a measurable financial loss.

Legal defense costs

Pays attorneys who specialize in professional disputes. Defending a claim can cost as much as settling one, and cover applies from the first allegation.

Misrepresentation claims

Covers claims that you overstated what your service would deliver or misrepresented your qualifications, where the client relied on those statements and lost money.

Work done by employees and contractors

Most policies extend to staff and, in many cases, subcontractors acting on your behalf, so a junior employee's error does not fall solely on you.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Bodily injury and property damage — those belong to general liability insurance
  • Fraud, dishonesty, or intentional wrongdoing once established
  • Fines and penalties imposed by regulators in many circumstances
  • Claims arising from work done before the retroactive date in your policy

How Much Does It Cost?

E&O pricing reflects how expensive your mistakes could be. As a general educational estimate for 2026, a solo consultant or freelancer commonly pays $600–$1,500 a year for $1 million of cover, small agencies pay $1,500–$4,000, and higher-risk professions such as financial advisers, architects, and technology providers with large clients can pay considerably more. Insurers weigh your revenue, services, client size, contract terms, and any past claims or complaints.

Who Needs It?

You need professional liability if clients pay for your judgment, deliverables, or advice and could plausibly lose money if something goes wrong. That includes consultants, designers, developers, accountants, real estate professionals, marketers, coaches, and IT providers. Many enterprise and government clients make E&O a written contract condition, and professional bodies in some fields require it outright. If your work product is the risk, this is your policy.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Map the services you actually sell. List every service, including informal extras, because cover is judged against the professional services described in your policy.
  2. Review client contracts. Note required limits, notice periods, and clauses where you accept liability, and flag anything broader than your cover.
  3. Choose a retroactive date carefully. Set it to when your current continuous cover began, or the start of your business, so earlier work is not left exposed.
  4. Compare claims-made terms. Check defense costs inside or outside the limit, consent-to-settle clauses, and what happens if you close or retire.
  5. Buy before you sign big clients. Binding cover before the contract starts keeps the work inside the policy period and satisfies client insurance checks.
  6. Report potential problems early. Tell your insurer about complaints or mistakes that might become claims. Early notice protects your right to cover.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Assuming general liability is enough

It excludes financial-loss claims from your professional work, so professionals who carry only general liability are uninsured for their main risk.

Letting cover lapse when work slows down

Claims-made cover must be active when the claim arrives. A gap can erase protection for years of past work.

Ignoring the retroactive date

Work performed before that date is excluded, which surprises owners who switch insurers and accept a reset date to save money.

Signing unlimited liability contracts

If you contractually accept more liability than your policy covers, the difference is yours to pay. Negotiate caps aligned with your limits.

The Bottom Line

If clients pay for what you know, professional liability is the policy that protects the business when that knowledge is challenged. Buy it before problems appear, keep it continuous, and treat the retroactive date as carefully as the limit.
