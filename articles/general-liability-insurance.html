General Liability Insurance for Small Businesses, Explained

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

General liability insurance is the policy most people picture when they think of business insurance. It responds when your business is accused of injuring a third party or damaging their property — the customer who slips on a wet floor, the ladder that falls onto a parked car, or the visitor whose laptop is knocked off your counter. Claims like these are common, unpredictable, and expensive to defend even when you did nothing wrong.

This guide explains the three main areas of cover, the exclusions owners most often misunderstand, and how limits such as $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate work in practice. You will also see realistic cost ranges for different trades and a step-by-step way to buy the right amount without paying for limits you do not need.

What It Covers

Bodily injury to third parties

Pays medical costs, settlements, and legal defense when a customer, vendor, or passerby is physically injured because of your operations, premises, or products.

Property damage to others

Covers the cost of repairing or replacing someone else's property that your business damages, for example a plumber flooding a client's home or a sign falling onto a car.

Personal and advertising injury

Responds to claims of libel, slander, defamation, false arrest, and certain copyright infringement in your advertising — a frequent and underrated exposure for businesses that market online.

Legal defense costs

Pays lawyers to defend covered claims, often in addition to the policy limit. Defense alone can run to tens of thousands of dollars, even for claims you ultimately win.

Medical payments cover

A small no-fault amount, often $5,000–$10,000, that can pay minor injury costs for visitors hurt at your premises regardless of who was at fault, helping settle small incidents quickly.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Injuries to your own employees — covered by workers compensation instead
  • Damage to your own building, stock, or equipment — that is commercial property cover
  • Mistakes in professional advice or services — that is professional liability
  • Auto accidents in business vehicles — that requires commercial auto insurance

How Much Does It Cost?

For 2026, a typical low-risk small business pays roughly $480–$1,100 a year for general liability as a general educational estimate, with many owners seeing monthly figures around $40–$90. Consultants and retailers sit at the lower end, while construction and other physical trades can pay $1,500–$3,000 or more because injuries are more likely and more severe. Insurers base the price mainly on your industry, revenue, location, claims history, and the limits you select.

Who Needs It?

You need general liability if anyone visits your premises, you work at client locations, you sell physical products, or you advertise your services — which is to say, almost every operating business. It is also the policy landlords and clients most frequently demand in contracts, typically requiring at least $1 million per occurrence. Even businesses that are never sued benefit, because the requirement alone can decide whether they win work.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Describe your operations accurately. Tell the insurer exactly what you do, where, and for whom. Misdescribed work is a common reason claims become disputes.
  2. Decide on limits. Most small businesses choose $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate; larger contracts or riskier work may justify more.
  3. Check the deductible and conditions. Confirm whether a deductible applies to property damage claims and what your duties are after an incident.
  4. Get quotes on identical terms. Ask two or three providers for the same limits, then compare exclusions and defense terms, not just price.
  5. Bind cover and store your certificate. Once paid, your policy is active. Save your certificate of insurance where clients and landlords can be sent it quickly.
  6. diarise your renewal date. Review revenue and activities before renewal so limits still fit, and shop around if the premium jumps sharply.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Thinking it covers professional mistakes

If a client loses money because your advice was wrong, general liability will not respond. That claim belongs to professional liability insurance.

Hiding part of your work from the application

Leaving out a side service, such as a café that also caters events, can leave that activity uninsured when something goes wrong.

Choosing limits to satisfy price alone

A $500,000 limit may breach a client contract requiring $1 million and leave a serious injury claim only partly paid.

Letting cover lapse between contracts

A gap means incidents in that window are uninsured, and continuous cover also matters for how some claims are triggered.

The Bottom Line

It is the foundation policy for a reason: affordable for most trades, demanded by most contracts, and aimed at the everyday accidents no business can schedule around. Get the limits right, describe your work honestly, and build the rest of your cover on top of it.
