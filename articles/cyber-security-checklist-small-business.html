The Small Business Cyber Security Checklist: 25 Practical Controls

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

Most small-business cyber incidents do not begin with a genius hacker. They begin with a reused password, an unpatched laptop, a fake invoice email, or a backup that turns out never to have run. The encouraging truth is that a short list of unglamorous controls stops the overwhelming majority of real attacks — and those controls are exactly what this checklist organises for you.

Written from day-to-day cyber security consulting practice, these 25 controls are grouped into identity, devices, email, data, payments, and people. Work through them in order; the first ten deliver the largest risk reduction for the least money. Each item states what done looks like, so you can honestly tick it off rather than vaguely intend it — and the finished checklist doubles as strong evidence for insurers and clients.

What It Covers

Identity controls 1–5

Unique passwords in a password manager, multi-factor authentication on email, banking, and remote access, separate admin accounts, prompt removal of leavers' access, and no shared logins.

Device controls 6–10

Automatic updates on every system, reputable endpoint protection, disk encryption on laptops, a properly configured firewall, and removal of unsupported software and hardware.

Email and web controls 11–14

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records on your domain, a spam and phishing filter, external-sender banners, and a rule that bank-detail changes are always verified by a phone call to a known number.

Data and backup controls 15–19

The 3-2-1 backup pattern with one copy offline or immutable, tested restores every quarter, least-privilege file access, automatic screen locks, and secure disposal of old drives and paper records.

People and payment controls 20–25

Short regular staff training with phishing examples, an incident contact card, dual approval for payments above a set amount, vendor access reviews, cyber insurance in place, and a written one-page response plan everyone can find.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Protection against every possible attack — checklists reduce risk dramatically but cannot eliminate it
  • Compliance certification: following this list supports, but does not by itself confer, formal standards or legal compliance
  • Safety for accounts you forget to include: shadow IT and personal devices used for work must be brought inside the controls
  • A substitute for incident response: even well-defended businesses need the response plan and insurance in items 24–25

How Much Does It Cost?

Most of this checklist costs time rather than money. As a general educational estimate for 2026, a small business can implement the core controls for $300–$1,500 a year using mainstream password managers, endpoint protection, and backup services for a handful of users, with the largest variable being paid help if you hire an IT provider to configure and verify everything. Compare that with the cost of one incident — professional response alone routinely starts in the thousands — and the checklist is the cheapest security money a small business spends.

Who Needs It?

Every small business with email, a bank account, customer data, or a website needs these controls — which is to say, every small business. Owners handling payments, health or legal data, or client system access should treat the full 25 as mandatory, because clients and insurers increasingly verify them through questionnaires. If you do nothing else, do items one to ten this week: identity and device basics are where real-world attacks most reliably die.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Start with identity this week. Enforce the password manager and multi-factor authentication on email and banking first; account takeover is the front door for most incidents.
  2. Automate updates and protection. Turn on automatic patching everywhere and confirm endpoint protection reports every device, including the laptop at someone's home.
  3. Fix email trust. Publish SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, and train the bank-change phone-call rule until it is reflex for anyone touching payments.
  4. Prove your backups. Run a real restore of one system and one file set this month. A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a control.
  5. Write the one-page response plan. Who to call, in what order, with insurer and IT numbers stored offline. Print it. Incidents do not schedule themselves around your systems being up.
  6. Review quarterly. Leavers, new tools, new vendors, and new bank accounts all change the picture. A one-hour quarterly review keeps the checklist true.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Treating MFA as optional for some accounts

Attackers choose the weakest account, which is usually the one someone exempted. Email and remote access MFA must be universal, no exceptions for owners.

Backing up to the same system

Backups connected to a compromised network get encrypted with everything else. At least one copy must be offline, immutable, or in a separate protected account.

Buying tools but never checking reports

Unmonitored security software quietly expiring or failing is a recurring finding after incidents. Assign a named person to check the dashboard weekly.

Training once and forgetting

Phishing changes monthly. Short, regular refreshers with real examples keep recognition sharp far better than an annual marathon session.
