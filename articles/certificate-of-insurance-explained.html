Certificate of Insurance (COI): What It Is and How to Get One Fast

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

Few documents move faster through a small business than a certificate of insurance. A new client will not let you start, a landlord will not hand over keys, and a marketplace will not release your listings until this one-page summary of your cover lands in their inbox. Yet many owners are fuzzy on what a COI actually proves — and, just as importantly, what it does not.

This guide decodes the standard ACORD 25 certificate section by section: the insurer and producer blocks, the policy table with its limits and dates, and the certificate holder box that causes most of the confusion. You will learn the difference between a certificate holder and an additional insured, why contracts ask for both, and the request workflow that gets a correct COI issued the same day instead of after three rounds of corrections.

What It Covers

Proof that cover exists

A COI summarises your active policies — types, insurers, policy numbers, effective dates, and limits — as evidence for a third party who needs to verify you are insured.

The limits snapshot

It shows the headline limits a client or landlord cares about, such as $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate general liability, at the date of issue.

Certificate holder details

The holder is the party receiving the certificate as evidence. Being a holder alone gives them no rights under your policy; it simply records who requested proof.

Additional insured endorsements

Separate from the certificate itself, an additional insured endorsement actually extends your policy to protect the named party for claims arising from your work, when a contract requires it.

Notice and special wording

Certificates can note contract-specific requirements and, where the underlying endorsement provides it, notice of cancellation provisions for the requesting party.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Any change to your actual policy — a certificate cannot amend, extend, or alter cover
  • Rights for the certificate holder, who gains no claim rights from the document alone
  • Cover for policies not listed, expired periods, or work outside your declared activities
  • A guarantee your insurer will notify the holder of cancellation, unless an endorsement in the policy provides that notice

How Much Does It Cost?

A COI itself is normally free: agents and insurer portals issue standard certificates at no charge as a general educational norm in 2026, and self-serve portals generate them in minutes. Costs arise only when the request requires the policy itself to change — adding an additional insured endorsement or a waiver of subrogation sometimes carries a small fee, commonly $25–$100 per endorsement with some insurers, and raising your limits to meet a contract costs whatever the higher premium is.

Who Needs It?

You need a COI whenever a client, landlord, lender, event organiser, licensing body, or marketplace asks for proof of insurance — which, for most trading businesses, is constantly. Keep your ability to produce one quickly: contracts are won and start dates kept on administrative speed as much as price. Any business signing contracts with insurance clauses should also understand the difference between sending a certificate and being contractually required to add endorsements behind it.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Collect the exact requirement in writing. Ask for the required policies, limits, holder name and address exactly as they must appear, and any endorsement wording, before you request the certificate.
  2. Request through your agent or portal. Self-serve portals are fastest for standard certificates; use your agent for anything involving endorsements or unusual wording.
  3. Match the holder details character for character. A misspelled holder name or old address is the most common reason certificates are rejected and reissued.
  4. Attach endorsements when required. If the contract requires additional insured or waiver status, the certificate alone is not enough — request the endorsements and send copies with it.
  5. Send and confirm receipt. Email the COI to the requester, confirm it satisfies their checklist, and file their acceptance with the contract.
  6. Diarise renewal for every live contract. When your policy renews, send fresh certificates to all active holders before the old ones expire and trigger compliance warnings.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Assuming a COI gives the holder cover

It does not. Owners who promise a client 'you are on our insurance' based only on a certificate create expectations the policy may not meet.

Editing a certificate yourself

Altering dates, limits, or wording on a COI is misrepresentation with serious consequences. Only the agent or insurer may issue or amend one.

Requesting at the last minute

Endorsements and underwriter approvals can take days. Request insurance documents when the contract is drafted, not the morning work starts.

Ignoring expiry monitoring

Expired certificates on file with clients lead to payment holds and suspended work. A simple tracker prevents the scramble.

The Bottom Line
