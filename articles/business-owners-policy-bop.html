Business Owner's Policy (BOP): The Bundle Most Small Businesses Start With

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

If insurers designed a starter kit for small businesses, this is it. A business owner's policy — universally shortened to BOP — bundles general liability and commercial property insurance into one policy, with one premium, one renewal date, and pricing that is usually lower than buying the two covers separately. For thousands of shops, offices, cafés, and small wholesalers, a BOP is the practical centre of their insurance programme.

Bundling brings simplicity, but it also brings eligibility rules and gaps you need to understand. This guide explains who qualifies for a BOP, what the standard bundle includes, the valuable extensions worth adding — business interruption, equipment breakdown, and hired and non-owned auto — and the point at which a growing business outgrows a BOP and needs individually written policies instead.

What It Covers

General liability cover

The same third-party bodily injury, property damage, and personal and advertising injury protection described in standalone liability policies, at limits you select.

Commercial property cover

Protection for your building if owned, plus contents, inventory, equipment, and tenant improvements against fire, theft, and other covered perils at your premises.

Business interruption cover

Most BOPs include loss-of-income cover that replaces revenue and pays continuing expenses when a covered property loss forces you to close or reduce trading.

Equipment breakdown

Commonly included or easily added, covering sudden mechanical and electrical failure of key equipment and, in many forms, resulting spoilage and interruption.

Useful built-in extensions

Small amounts of cover for outdoor signage, money and securities, employee dishonesty in some forms, and valuable papers, bundled at levels designed for typical small businesses.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Professional liability for mistakes in your services or advice
  • Workers compensation for employee injuries — always a separate policy
  • Commercial auto cover for owned vehicles, beyond limited hired and non-owned extensions
  • Cyber incidents beyond the small data-breach add-ons some BOPs offer

How Much Does It Cost?

A BOP rewards being a straightforward, lower-risk business. As a general educational estimate for 2026, many eligible small businesses pay $1,200–$3,000 a year for a BOP, with small offices and boutiques at the lower end and restaurants and busier retail at the higher end. That bundled price is often 10–20 percent below the combined cost of separate liability and property policies with similar limits. Eligibility, however, is the catch: insurers restrict BOPs by industry, size, revenue, and premises risk.

Who Needs It?

You are a strong BOP candidate if you run a small, low-to-moderate risk business from a defined premises — retail, offices, small restaurants, salons, wholesalers, and many trades with workshops. You likely need one if your landlord requires liability and contents cover in a single, easily evidenced policy. Businesses with large revenues, multiple complex locations, heavy manufacturing, or unusual liability exposures are usually moved to a commercial package or separate policies instead.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Confirm your business is eligible. Give your exact trade, revenue, payroll, and premises details; eligibility decisions turn on these, and mistakes surface at claim time.
  2. Set liability limits first. Choose the liability limits your contracts require, commonly $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate, before fine-tuning property values.
  3. Value property honestly. Schedule your contents, stock, and improvements at replacement cost so the property half of the bundle performs when you claim.
  4. Add the extensions you truly need. Consider interruption indemnity periods of at least twelve months, equipment breakdown, and any trade-specific additions such as food spoilage.
  5. Compare bundle against separate quotes. Occasionally separate policies win on cover or price for unusual businesses. A quick comparison keeps the bundle honest.
  6. Note what remains to buy. List the separate policies you still need — workers compensation, professional liability, commercial auto, cyber — and arrange them alongside.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Assuming a BOP covers everything

The name sounds complete, but a BOP excludes professional mistakes, employee injuries, and owned vehicles. Owners discover these gaps at the worst possible moment.

Underestimating interruption time

Rebuilding after a fire takes longer than expected. A six-month indemnity period that expires mid-rebuild stops paying while expenses continue.

Accepting default property limits

Bundles arrive with standard contents limits that may be far below your real stock and fit-out value. Check the schedule line by line.

Not telling the insurer about changes

A kitchen added to a shop, or storage moved to a second unit, changes the risk. Undeclared changes can complicate or reduce claims.

The Bottom Line

For the businesses it suits, a BOP is the simplest good decision in commercial insurance: two essential covers, one sensible price. Just remember it is the centre of your programme, not the whole of it.
