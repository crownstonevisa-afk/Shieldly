Data Breach Response Plan: What to Do in the First 72 Hours

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

When a breach is discovered, the clock starts — on evidence that degrades, on legal notification duties, and on customer trust. The businesses that come through breaches best are rarely the ones with the biggest budgets; they are the ones with a plan written before the bad day, so the first 72 hours follow a script instead of an argument. This guide is that script, scaled for a small business.

We move hour by hour through containment, evidence preservation, and the calls that must happen in the right order — insurer, forensic specialists, legal counsel — then into assessing whose data was involved, meeting US state notification obligations, communicating honestly with customers, and recovering systems safely. Keep this guide with your one-page plan, the insurer hotline number stored offline, and the names of the people authorised to act.

What It Covers

Containment in the first hours

Isolating affected systems, disabling compromised accounts, and blocking attacker access without destroying evidence — stopping the bleeding while preserving the trail.

Professional investigation

Forensic specialists determine how entry happened, what was accessed or taken, and whether the attacker remains present, which drives every legal and notification decision that follows.

Legal and regulatory duties

US state breach laws require notification to affected individuals and sometimes regulators within defined windows when personal data is involved, with content and timing set by each state's statute.

Customer notification and support

Clear letters or emails explaining what happened, what data was involved, what you are doing, and what customers should do, often with credit monitoring offered for sensitive data.

Recovery and hardening

Rebuilding systems cleanly, resetting credentials everywhere, closing the entry point, and monitoring for return — recovery is complete only when the original hole is provably shut.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Public statements made before facts are established — speculate early and you will correct yourself publicly later
  • Deleting or wiping systems to 'clean up', which destroys forensic evidence and can breach legal duties
  • Direct negotiation with extortion criminals by untrained staff, which insurers and law enforcement strongly discourage
  • Promising customers outcomes — such as guaranteed identity protection — that no one can honestly assure

How Much Does It Cost?

The plan itself costs nothing to write, but executing it has real costs, which is why cyber insurance matters. As a general educational estimate for 2026, professional forensic investigation for a small business commonly runs $5,000–$30,000 depending on scope, legal guidance $3,000–$15,000, and notification and monitoring for affected customers varies by record count. With cyber cover in place, these become the insurer's panel costs within your limit; without it, they arrive as emergency invoices during your worst week.

Who Needs It?

Any business holding personal data — customer lists, payment details, employee records, health or financial information — needs this plan, and in most US states the law assumes you have one when it sets notification deadlines. If you would need more than an hour to answer 'who do we call first?', the plan is overdue. Solo operators need a lightweight version too: insurer number, IT help, and a notification checklist stored somewhere that survives their own laptop failing.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Write the one-page plan before anything happens. Names, roles, insurer hotline, IT and legal contacts, and the first ten actions, stored printed and offline as well as digitally.
  2. Hour 0–4: contain and call. Isolate affected systems, preserve logs and images, change exposed credentials from a clean device, and call your insurer's incident line before engaging other vendors.
  3. Hour 4–24: scope with professionals. Let forensics establish entry point, dwell time, and data involved. Do not announce specifics until this picture firms up.
  4. Day 1–3: decide notification duties with counsel. Map affected individuals to their state laws, prepare notices and support offers, and brief whoever answers phones and email.
  5. Communicate once, clearly, and honestly. Tell affected people what happened, what data, what you have done, and what they should do. Plain language protects trust better than legal fog.
  6. Recover, harden, and record lessons. Rebuild from clean sources, close the entry point, reset everything, and write the post-incident review while memories are fresh.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Paying or promising anything to attackers directly

Untrained contact can breach sanctions, void insurance conditions, and rarely ends the demands. Route all such contact through insurer-appointed specialists.

Restarting systems to get trading at any cost

Reconnecting unrebuilt systems invites the attacker — often still present — straight back in, converting one incident into two.

Notifying everyone before knowing anything

Over-notification to unaffected people causes panic and cost; under-notification breaches law. Forensic scoping is what separates the two correctly.

Treating the incident as over when systems return

Legal duties, monitoring, customer questions, and hardening run for weeks after recovery. Staff the tail of the incident, not just the fire.

The Bottom Line

A breach tests preparation, not intentions. Write the plan, store it offline, know the first phone call — and the worst week in your business's life becomes a managed incident with an end date.
