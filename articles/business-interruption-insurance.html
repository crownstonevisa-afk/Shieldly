Business Interruption Insurance: Keeping Income Alive After a Disaster

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

Picture your business after a serious fire or flood. The property policy will eventually repair the damage — but rent, loan payments, and key salaries continue while you cannot trade, and customers drift to competitors every week you are closed. Business interruption insurance exists for that gap: it replaces lost income and pays essential continuing costs while you recover from a covered physical loss.

This guide explains how interruption cover is calculated, the difference between gross profit and revenue methods, and why the indemnity period you choose matters more than the premium you pay. You will also learn about extensions for supplier failures, utility outages, and civil authority closures, which decided many real claims in recent years.

What It Covers

Lost net income

Replaces the profit you would have earned during the closure, calculated from your accounts and trading trends, so a disaster does not turn a good year into a loss.

Continuing fixed costs

Pays expenses that continue while you are shut, such as rent, loan interest, insurance premiums, and retained staff wages where covered.

Extra expense cover

Funds reasonable additional costs to get trading again faster or from temporary premises — renting equipment, expedited repairs, or notifying customers of a new location.

Supplier and utility extensions

Optional extensions respond when a key supplier's premises are damaged or a utility failure interrupts your operations, even though your own property is untouched.

Civil authority cover

Pays when government action following nearby damage — a cordoned street after a neighbouring fire, for example — prevents access to your premises for a stated period.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Closures with no underlying physical damage at an insured location, unless a specific extension applies
  • Pandemic and communicable disease closures under most standard forms, which generally require a specific buy-back if available
  • Losses beyond the indemnity period you selected, even if recovery is still incomplete
  • Voluntary closures or quiet trading caused by economic downturn rather than an insured event

How Much Does It Cost?

Interruption cover is usually priced as part of a property package rather than sold alone, and its cost follows the income figure you declare. As a general educational estimate for 2026, a small business might pay $300–$900 a year to include meaningful interruption cover within a package, rising for restaurants and manufacturers whose income depends entirely on one premises. The declared gross income figure must be accurate: underdeclare it and the insurer can reduce claims in proportion, a condition known as average.

Who Needs It?

You need interruption cover if your income depends on a physical place, specialist equipment, or stock that cannot be replaced overnight — which covers most retailers, hospitality businesses, clinics, workshops, and manufacturers. Ask one question: if this premises burned tonight, how many months of costs could you fund with no income? If the honest answer is fewer than it would take to rebuild and restock, interruption cover is the policy that closes that gap.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Calculate your true annual gross income. Use your accounts and your accountant's definition required by the policy wording, including trends, not last year's number by habit.
  2. Choose an indemnity period from a rebuild timeline. Ask a contractor how long demolition, permits, rebuilding, refitting, and restocking would realistically take, then add a margin — twelve months is a floor, not a target, for many.
  3. List your continuing costs. Rent, finance, key salaries, and service contracts show what must be paid while closed and help size the cover correctly.
  4. Consider the extensions that fit your reality. Single-supplier dependence, flood-prone utilities, or a location that could be cordoned off all argue for specific extensions.
  5. Prepare a simple continuity plan. Knowing your temporary premises options and priority suppliers in advance speeds both recovery and your claim.
  6. Keep financial records backed up off-site. Claims are settled from evidence. Cloud accounting and backed-up sales records make proving lost income far simpler.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Declaring a low income figure to cut the premium

The saving is small and the penalty is automatic: claims are scaled down by the same proportion you underdeclared, exactly when you can least afford it.

Picking six months to save money

Permits and construction rarely run to best-case timetables. When the indemnity period ends, payments stop even if the doors are still closed.

Assuming any closure qualifies

A quiet month, a road closure for planned works, or a voluntary refurbishment are not insured events. Physical damage under the policy is the usual trigger.

Forgetting payroll decisions

Retaining trained staff is often the difference between reopening and closing forever. Decide in advance which roles the policy should protect.

The Bottom Line

Property insurance rebuilds your walls; interruption insurance rebuilds your business. Declare your income honestly, choose an indemnity period based on reality rather than optimism, and the two policies together give you a genuine chance to reopen.
