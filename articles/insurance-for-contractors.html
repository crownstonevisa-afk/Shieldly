Insurance for Contractors: Cover for Builders, Trades, and Job Sites

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

Contracting concentrates risk into hard physical work: people can be hurt, property can be damaged, projects run on other people's premises, and your tools travel everywhere with you. Contractors are also among the most-checked businesses in insurance — general contractors, property managers, and homeowners routinely demand certificates, additional insured status, and waivers of subrogation before you set foot on site.

This guide assembles the contractor's full toolkit: general liability with completed operations, workers compensation, commercial auto, tools and equipment cover, builders risk for projects in progress, and surety bonds where contracts require them. It explains the paperwork language you will meet in subcontracts and how to keep your certificates, endorsements, and subcontractor checks organised enough that insurance never delays a start date.

What It Covers

General liability with completed operations

Covers third-party injury and property damage during and after your work — the burst pipe a week after installation, or the finished deck that fails — within the products-completed operations part of the policy.

Workers compensation and employer liability

Required cover for your crew's injuries, priced by trade classification, with roofing, framing, and other height work at the expensive end for understandable reasons.

Tools and equipment (inland marine)

Covers your tools, plant, and equipment against theft and damage wherever the work is — on site, in your van, or in storage — where property policies fall short.

Builders risk insurance

Protects the project itself while under construction: materials, work in progress, and sometimes soft costs, against fire, theft, vandalism, and weather for the course of construction.

Commercial auto and hired/non-owned cover

Covers work vehicles at business limits and extends liability thinking to rented trucks and employees' vehicles used for supply runs and site visits.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • Defective workmanship itself — liability cover pays for damage your faulty work causes, not the cost of redoing your own work
  • Surety bond obligations inside an insurance policy — bonds are a separate product guaranteeing your performance or payment
  • Injuries to subcontractors' employees — their own workers compensation responds, which is why you verify it
  • Tools left unsecured contrary to policy conditions, such as overnight in unlocked vehicles where warranties apply

How Much Does It Cost?

Contractors pay more for insurance because their claims can be severe. As a general educational estimate for 2026, a solo handyman might assemble a basic programme for $2,500–$5,000 a year, a small general contractor with a crew and vehicles $12,000–$30,000, and roofing or structural firms considerably more, driven mainly by workers compensation rates and liability class. Subcontract terms can also force higher limits — $2 million aggregate is common — and umbrella policies are a routine way to reach them.

Who Needs It?

You need this cover if you work on other people's property, employ or subcontract labour, use vehicles and tools for work, or bid for jobs with general contractors, municipalities, platforms, or commercial clients. In practice that is every trading contractor. Many states also tie contractor licensing to proof of liability and workers compensation, and a lapsed policy can suspend both your licence and your right to be on site.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Read subcontract insurance clauses first. Extract required limits, additional insured, waiver, and notice clauses before pricing the job, and cost them into your bid.
  2. Assemble the core programme. Bind general liability, workers compensation, and commercial auto, then add tools cover and builders risk per project.
  3. Set up certificate workflows. Use your agent or portal to issue certificates fast, with endorsements that match each contract, and keep a log per job.
  4. Vet every subcontractor. Collect their certificates, verify cover is current directly where stakes are high, and diarise expiry dates during the project.
  5. Classify payroll precisely. Separate clerical, supervisory, and trade payroll correctly; misclassification is expensive in both directions for contractors.
  6. Review limits against your largest job. Your programme should satisfy the biggest contract you realistically pursue, with an umbrella filling any gap.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Signing additional insured clauses blindly

Endorsements change whose insurance pays first and how much of your limit is shared. Sign with your agent's eyes on the wording.

Hiring uninsured subcontractors to save money

If their worker is hurt or their work causes damage, the claim climbs to you. The saving is dwarfed by one incident.

Assuming tools are covered by the van policy

Vehicle insurance covers the vehicle. Tools need inland marine cover, and theft conditions — locks, alarms, overnight rules — must be followed.

Letting certificates expire mid-project

General contractors monitor expiries and can stop work and payment. Track renewals across your whole programme and your subcontractors'.

The Bottom Line

For contractors, insurance is both protection and passport: it guards the business and unlocks the better jobs. Keep it current, keep the paperwork tight, and price its requirements into every bid.
