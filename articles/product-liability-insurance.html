Product Liability Insurance: If What You Sell Hurts Someone

By Muhammad Sameer, Cyber Security Consultant • Updated October 17, 2026 • Educational guide for US small business owners

Every product that leaves your hands carries a promise: used as intended, it will not hurt anyone. When that promise is challenged — a food product that triggers an allergic reaction, a charger that overheats, a toy part that detaches — product liability insurance is the cover that defends you and pays covered settlements. Crucially, you do not have to be the manufacturer to be sued; importers, distributors, and retailers are regularly named in the same claim.

This guide explains the three legal grounds most claims use — manufacturing defects, design defects, and failure to warn — and how cover responds at each stage of the supply chain. You will see why insurers scrutinize product categories so closely, what a recall can and cannot be insured, and how to present your quality controls so underwriters view your products favourably.

What It Covers

Defective product claims

Covers bodily injury and property damage caused by a manufacturing flaw, contamination, or an item that deviated from its intended design and harmed a user.

Design defect claims

Responds when a product's design itself is alleged to be unreasonably dangerous, even though every unit was made exactly to specification.

Failure-to-warn claims

Covers allegations that instructions, labels, or warnings were missing or inadequate, so a foreseeable user did not know how to use the product safely.

Legal defense across the supply chain

Pays defense costs and manages claims where several parties are sued together, with insurers coordinating contributions from manufacturers, importers, and sellers.

Products-completed operations cover

Product liability is commonly written within general liability as products-completed operations cover, responding after the product has left your premises and your control.

What It Does Not Cover

A policy only pays for the risks named in its wording, subject to limits, deductibles, conditions, and exclusions. The following are commonly excluded or require separate cover, and assuming otherwise is one of the most expensive misunderstandings owners make:

  • The cost of recalling and replacing the product itself, unless you buy specific product recall cover
  • Damage to the defective product itself — cover concerns injury and damage the product causes to people and other property
  • Knowingly selling a product you knew was defective or illegal to sell
  • Contractual penalties and pure economic loss with no injury or property damage, in most standard forms

How Much Does It Cost?

Product risk drives price more than business size. As a general educational estimate for 2026, a US retailer selling low-risk finished goods might pay $600–$1,500 a year for product liability within a liability package, a small manufacturer of consumer goods $2,500–$7,500, and makers of higher-risk items such as supplements, children's products, or electrical goods considerably more, if cover is available at all. Insurers examine ingredients, components, sourcing, testing, and claims history in detail.

Who Needs It?

You need product liability if you make, import, rebrand, assemble, or sell physical products — including selling other companies' products under your own label, which the law commonly treats as manufacturing. Online sellers are included: marketplaces increasingly require proof of product liability cover above stated sales thresholds. Services businesses that supply installed parts, from plumbers to IT hardware resellers, also carry product exposure inside their completed work.

How to Choose and Buy, Step by Step

  1. Map your place in the supply chain. Identify whether you manufacture, import, rebrand, or resell each product, and who else could share or carry the risk.
  2. Document testing and compliance. Keep certificates, lab results, standards compliance, and supplier agreements in one file ready for underwriters and platforms.
  3. Review labels and warnings honestly. Compare your warnings against known risks and regulations for your category, and fix gaps before an insurer or a claimant finds them.
  4. Confirm supplier insurance. Require manufacturers and importers to carry their own product liability and name you as an additional insured where appropriate.
  5. Choose limits for your category, not your size. A tiny seller of a risky product needs higher limits than a large seller of a harmless one. Price limits against plausible injury claims.
  6. Plan batch and lot tracking. Being able to identify exactly which units are affected limits recalls, supports defenses, and demonstrates control to insurers.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Assuming the manufacturer carries all the risk

Plaintiffs sue everyone in reach. If your supplier is overseas, uninsured, or insolvent, your business may carry the claim alone.

White-labelling without extra diligence

Putting your brand on a product makes its risks your brand's risks. Test and document as rigorously as if you had designed it.

Treating recall costs as covered

Standard product liability pays for harm a product causes, not for pulling it from shelves. Businesses with real recall exposure need specific recall cover.

Changing suppliers or ingredients quietly

Material changes can alter the risk you insured. Tell your insurer before the new stock ships, not after a claim arrives.

The Bottom Line

Selling products means standing behind them in court as well as in the marketplace. Know your place in the chain, document your controls, and carry limits matched to the harm your category could cause — then product liability becomes a quiet enabler of growth, including onto the biggest platforms.
